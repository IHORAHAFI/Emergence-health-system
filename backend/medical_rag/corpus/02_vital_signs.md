## What vital signs are
Vital signs are basic measurements of how the body is working: body temperature, heart rate (pulse), breathing rate (respiratory rate), blood pressure and oxygen saturation (SpO2). Health workers compare them with normal ranges for the person's age and look at how they change over time.

## Typical adult resting values
Body temperature about 36.1 to 37.2 °C. Heart rate 60 to 100 beats per minute. Breathing rate 12 to 20 breaths per minute. Blood pressure below 120/80 mmHg is considered normal. Oxygen saturation 95 to 100% in healthy people at rest. Values can differ in athletes, older people, pregnancy and people with long-term conditions.

## Why one value is not a diagnosis
A single abnormal reading can come from movement, pain, stress, recent exercise, a loose sensor or a device error. Repeat the measurement at rest. Health workers decide what a reading means together with symptoms and history.

## Normal resting heart rate
Adults: 60 to 100 beats per minute (bpm). Well-trained athletes may be 40 to 60 bpm. Children have faster heart rates than adults. Approximate resting ranges: newborns about 100 to 160 bpm, children 1 to 10 years about 70 to 130 bpm, children over 10 similar to adults. Different references give slightly different ranges.

## Fast or slow heart rate
Tachycardia means a resting adult heart rate above 100 bpm; it can be caused by fever, dehydration, pain, anxiety, anaemia, infection, some medicines or heart rhythm problems. Bradycardia means below 60 bpm; it can be normal in fit people but may be caused by heart conduction problems, some medicines or low thyroid function.

## When to seek help
Seek emergency help if an abnormal heart rate comes with chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath or confusion. Seek medical advice if you often feel your heart racing, pounding or skipping.

## How blood pressure is measured
Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against artery walls. It is written as two numbers in mmHg, for example 120/80. The top number (systolic) is the pressure when the heart beats; the bottom number (diastolic) is the pressure when the heart rests between beats. It is measured with a cuff on the upper arm after sitting quietly for a few minutes.

## Normal, high and low
Normal: below 120/80 mmHg. High blood pressure (hypertension) is usually diagnosed when readings are 140/90 mmHg or higher on two different days. Low blood pressure (hypotension) is generally below 90/60 mmHg.

## Difference between hypertension and hypotension
Hypertension usually causes no symptoms but over time damages the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes, raising the risk of heart attack and stroke. Hypotension can be normal in some healthy people, but when it is new or causes dizziness, fainting, blurred vision, confusion or cold clammy skin it may be caused by dehydration, bleeding, severe infection, heart problems or medicines.

## When to seek help
Get emergency help for very high blood pressure (for example 180/120 mmHg or above) with chest pain, severe headache, weakness, vision changes or confusion, or for low blood pressure with fainting, confusion or signs of shock.

## Normal temperature
Normal body temperature is about 36.1 to 37.2 °C and changes a little during the day. A temperature of 38.0 °C or higher is usually considered a fever.

## What fever means
Fever is the body's normal response to infection or inflammation. Common causes include viral infections, malaria, bacterial infections such as pneumonia or urinary tract infection, and some other illnesses.

## Low body temperature
A temperature below 35 °C (hypothermia) can be dangerous, especially in babies and older people, and needs urgent care.

## When to seek help
Seek medical care for fever in a baby under 3 months, fever lasting more than 2 to 3 days, or fever where malaria is possible. Seek emergency help for fever with a stiff neck, confusion, seizures, difficulty breathing, a rash that does not fade when pressed, or inability to drink.

## Normal breathing rate
Adults at rest breathe about 12 to 20 times per minute. Count breaths for a full minute while the person is resting.

## Fast breathing in children
WHO child-health guidance treats these rates as fast breathing: 60 or more breaths per minute in babies under 2 months, 50 or more in children aged 2 to 12 months, and 40 or more in children aged 1 to 5 years. Fast breathing in a child with cough can be a sign of pneumonia and needs assessment by a health worker.

## When to seek help
Get emergency help for breathing that is very fast or very slow, chest in-drawing in a child, grunting, blue lips, inability to speak in full sentences, or drowsiness.

## What SpO2 means
SpO2 is the percentage of haemoglobin in the blood that is carrying oxygen. It is measured with a pulse oximeter clipped on a finger. In healthy people at rest it is usually 95 to 100%.

## What low oxygen saturation means
A reading below about 94% is lower than normal and should be checked. Below 90% is low and usually needs urgent medical care. Low oxygen can be caused by pneumonia, asthma, COPD, COVID-19, heart failure, blood clots in the lungs and other conditions. Some people with long-term lung disease have a lower target set by their doctor.

## Getting a reliable reading
Keep the hand warm and still, remove nail polish, and wait until the number is steady. Cold fingers, movement, bright light and poor circulation can give wrong readings. Studies have shown pulse oximeters can overestimate oxygen levels in people with darker skin, so symptoms matter as much as the number.

## When to seek help
Get emergency help for low SpO2 together with difficulty breathing, blue lips, confusion or drowsiness, even if the device reading seems acceptable.

## What NEWS2 is
NEWS2 is a scoring system used by health workers. It gives points for how far breathing rate, oxygen saturation, use of oxygen, blood pressure, pulse, level of consciousness and temperature are from normal, and adds them up.

## What the score means
A higher score means a higher chance that the person is becoming seriously unwell. In general, 0 to 4 is low risk, 5 or 6 (or a single parameter scoring 3) is medium risk and needs urgent clinical review, and 7 or more is high risk and needs emergency assessment.

## Limits
NEWS2 was designed for adults (not children or pregnant women) and supports, but does not replace, a health professional's judgement. In this system the score is decision support only; clinicians confirm or override it.
