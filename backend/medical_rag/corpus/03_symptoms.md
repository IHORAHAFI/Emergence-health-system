## Possible causes
Chest pain has many causes. Some are serious: heart attack, angina (reduced blood flow to the heart), a blood clot in the lung (pulmonary embolism), a torn aorta or a collapsed lung. Others are less dangerous: muscle strain, heartburn or acid reflux, inflammation of the chest-wall cartilage, anxiety or panic attacks, and chest infections.

## Dangerous warning signs
Pressure, squeezing or heaviness in the chest; pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck or back; shortness of breath; sweating, nausea or light-headedness; sudden sharp pain with breathlessness; chest pain after an injury.

## When emergency help is needed
Treat new, severe or crushing chest pain, or chest pain with any warning sign, as an emergency and request an ambulance at once. Do not drive yourself. It is not possible to tell the cause of chest pain safely without a medical examination and tests such as an ECG.

## Possible causes
Asthma, chest infections such as pneumonia, COPD, heart failure, severe allergic reactions, anaemia, anxiety, blood clots in the lungs and COVID-19 can all cause difficulty breathing.

## Warning signs
Breathing that is very hard or fast, being unable to speak full sentences, blue or grey lips, chest pain, confusion or drowsiness, swelling of the face or throat, and in children chest in-drawing or grunting.

## When to seek help
Severe or sudden difficulty breathing is an emergency: request help immediately. Seek prompt medical care for breathlessness that is new, getting worse, or comes with fever or cough.

## Possible causes
Most headaches are tension-type headaches or migraine. Others are linked to dehydration, lack of sleep, fever and infections such as malaria, eye strain, high blood pressure, or overuse of pain medicines. Rarely, headache is a sign of a serious problem such as meningitis, bleeding in the brain or stroke.

## Warning signs
A sudden, very severe headache ("the worst ever"); headache with fever and stiff neck, rash or confusion; headache after a head injury; headache with weakness, numbness, vision loss or difficulty speaking; a new headache in pregnancy, especially with swelling or vision changes.

## When to seek help
Seek emergency help for any warning sign. See a health worker for headaches that are frequent, getting worse, or that wake you from sleep.

## Possible causes
Fever is a sign that the body is fighting an infection or inflammation. In Rwanda and other malaria areas, malaria must be considered and tested for promptly. Other causes include viral infections such as flu, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, typhoid and many others.

## What usually helps
Rest, drinking plenty of fluids and light clothing. Ask a health worker or pharmacist which fever medicine and dose is suitable, especially for children.

## When to seek help
Get a malaria test for any fever where malaria is possible. Seek urgent care for fever in a baby under 3 months, fever with confusion, fits, stiff neck, difficulty breathing, a rash that does not fade, repeated vomiting, or a child who cannot drink or breastfeed.

## Possible causes
Most coughs are caused by colds and other viral infections and get better within 3 weeks. Other causes include asthma, pneumonia, tuberculosis, COPD, smoking, acid reflux and some blood-pressure medicines.

## Warning signs
Coughing up blood; difficulty breathing; chest pain; high fever; a cough lasting more than 2 weeks with weight loss or night sweats (a possible sign of tuberculosis); fast breathing in a child.

## When to seek help
See a health worker for a cough lasting more than 2 weeks or with any warning sign. Get emergency help for severe breathing difficulty or coughing up large amounts of blood.

## Possible causes
Dizziness can be caused by standing up quickly, dehydration, low blood sugar, low blood pressure, anaemia, inner-ear problems (vertigo), anxiety, some medicines, and less often heart rhythm problems or stroke.

## Warning signs
Dizziness with chest pain, a racing or very slow heartbeat, fainting, sudden weakness or numbness, difficulty speaking, double vision or a severe headache.

## When to seek help
Get emergency help for any warning sign. Lie down if you feel faint. See a health worker if dizziness keeps coming back.

## Possible causes
Most vomiting and diarrhoea is caused by infections from contaminated food or water (viruses, bacteria or parasites). Other causes include food poisoning, some medicines, cholera during outbreaks, and conditions such as appendicitis.

## The main danger: dehydration
Diarrhoea and vomiting cause loss of water and salts. Signs of dehydration are thirst, dry mouth, little or dark urine, sunken eyes, dizziness, and in children unusual sleepiness or no tears. Oral rehydration solution (ORS) replaces lost water and salts; WHO also recommends zinc for children with diarrhoea.

## When to seek help
Seek urgent care for blood in the stool, signs of dehydration, vomiting that stops you keeping fluids down, high fever, severe abdominal pain, very large amounts of watery stool, or diarrhoea in a young baby.

## Possible causes
Common causes are indigestion, gastritis, constipation, gastroenteritis, menstrual cramps and urinary tract infection. Some causes need urgent treatment: appendicitis, a blocked bowel, gallbladder or pancreas inflammation, kidney stones, and ectopic pregnancy.

## Warning signs
Severe or worsening pain; pain that moves to the lower right side; a hard, tender belly; vomiting blood or black stools; fever with pain; pain in pregnancy or with vaginal bleeding; pain after an injury.

## When to seek help
Seek emergency care for severe pain or any warning sign. See a health worker for pain that lasts more than a few days or keeps coming back.

## Possible causes
Tiredness is very common and often linked to poor sleep, stress, overwork or recent illness. Medical causes include anaemia, diabetes, thyroid problems, infections (including malaria, HIV and tuberculosis), depression, heart or kidney disease, and some medicines.

## Sudden weakness is different
Sudden weakness or numbness of the face, arm or leg, especially on one side, can be a stroke and is an emergency.

## When to seek help
See a health worker for tiredness lasting more than 2 weeks, or with weight loss, fever, night sweats, breathlessness or low mood. Get emergency help for sudden one-sided weakness.

## Possible causes
Rashes can come from allergies, eczema, insect bites, fungal infections, scabies, viral infections such as measles or chickenpox, and reactions to medicines.

## Warning signs
A rash that does not fade when a glass is pressed on it (can be meningitis or blood infection); rash with fever and feeling very unwell; swelling of the lips, face or tongue or difficulty breathing (severe allergy); blistering or peeling skin, especially after starting a new medicine.

## When to seek help
Get emergency help for any warning sign. See a health worker for rashes that spread, are painful or last more than a few days.
